# AI tells and craft: fiction, screenwriting, poetry, lyrics

Scope: craft quality, not detector evasion. Dates are publish dates. "Possibly outdated" = pre-2025.

## Key questions

### Takeaway
Fiction from current LLMs converges on a small set of names, settings, themes and endings, and states its meaning outright. Diversity research is strong (2025-2026); editor and poetry evidence is thinner and partly older.

### Cited Findings

**Homogeneity (research)**
- Across 20,000 stories from four current LLMs (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, OLMo), 11 words appear in 88.3% of stories, with little variation between models. Recurring names: Elias, Mara, Elara; settings and professions: lighthouse, keeper, clockmaker, librarian, baker, mayor. Hamilton & Mimno, arXiv 2605.26492, 26 May 2026 (preprint, no venue stated). [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26492); [Cornell Chronicle](https://news.cornell.edu/node/333804), 19 Oct 2026. The authors suspect mode collapse and a likely origin in the WildChat dataset; cause not confirmed.
- Claude-generated stories were titled "The Lighthouse Keeper's Secret" in 56% of cases, per a search summary of the paper. Not verified in the full text. [Unite.ai](https://www.unite.ai/why-does-ai-love-writing-about-lighthouse-keepers/)
- StoryScope (structural study, per a TDS summary, not verified in the paper): AI stories state their theme explicitly 77% of the time vs 52% for human stories; 79% have no subplot vs 57% of human stories. [TDS summary](https://towardsdatascience.com/what-makes-ai-written-stories-feel-off)
- "We're Different, We're the Same": LLM responses to standardized creativity tests are more similar to each other than human responses are to each other. Wenger & Kenett, arXiv 2501.19361, 31 Jan 2025; no numbers in the abstract. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.19361)
- Artificial Hivemind: intra-model repetition and strong inter-model similarity on 26K open-ended queries (Infinity-Chat). Jiang et al., arXiv 2510.22954, 27 Oct 2025; listed as NeurIPS 2025 D&B. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.22954)
- NoveltyBench (COLM 2025): 20 models produce less diversity than human writers; larger models in a family are often less diverse. In-context regeneration (asking for a different answer after seeing previous ones) worked best of the mitigations tested. Mar/Apr 2025 preprint arXiv 2504.05228. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05228)

**Poetry (research, possibly outdated)**
- Porter & Machery: readers rated AI poems (ChatGPT 3.5, 2024) as human-written more often than real human poems; readers identified AI poems at 46.6% accuracy. Sci Rep 2024. Possibly outdated model. Critics (Ernest Davis) say claims overreach. Follow-up (696 participants): poems labeled AI got lower ratings. [Press release](https://www.natureasia.com/ja-jp/nature/pr-highlights/15091); [Marginal Revolution](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2024/11/ai-generated-poetry-is-indistinguishable-from-human-written-poetry-and-is-rated-more-favorably.html)

**Editors and magazines (2023-2026)**
- Clarkesworld closed submissions in Feb 2023 after a flood of AI stories; more than 500 "authors" were banned by mid-Feb 2023. Possibly outdated. [Guardian via PressReader](https://www.pressreader.com/usa/the-guardian-usa/20230222/281539410143635)
- Editor's detection clues (Clarkesworld, 2023): odd word choice, odd sentence construction, matching titles across submissions. He also said legitimate work gets flagged and detection tools are unreliable. [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2023/02/22/flood-chatgpt-ai-generated-content-science-fiction-writing-clarkesworld-stops-submissions/)
- Reckoning Press: no work created or edited in any part by an LLM. Brevity: no AI-produced work. [Reckoning](https://reckoning.press/llm-ai-statement/); [Brevity](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/25861603-does-brevity-accept-ai-generated-work)
- Vine Leaves Press: AI-written submissions "will be rejected." Autumn House Press forbids AI-generated or AI-supported work. [Poets & Writers, 2026](https://pw.org/node/1170676)
- Poets & Writers 2026: editors are wary of AI detectors. Translunar Travelers Lounge (Jul 2026) is adjusting slush process. [TTLL](https://translunartravelerslounge.com/2026/07/16/ttls-statement-on-ai-generated-stories/)
- Wikipedia "Signs of AI writing" (WikiProject AI Cleanup): lists word and structure tells; says patterns are indicators, not proof, and that several together matter more than one. Historical list includes em-dash overuse. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing); [TechCrunch, 20 Nov 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/11/20/the-best-guide-to-spotting-ai-writing-comes-from-wikipedia/)

**Craft (working writers, limited)**
- Le Guin (Steering the Craft, 2015; secondary summaries): show-don't-tell is a maxim, not a law; exposition is fine if it moves the story; read dialogue aloud to test it. [Brain Pickings](https://www.brainpickings.org/2016/03/10/ursula-k-le-guin-writing-advice/)
- Jerry Jenkins guide, Dec 2025: showing through physical detail and dialogue. [Jenkins](https://jerryjenkins.com/show-dont-tell/)
- Dialogue guide (undated): characters rarely say what they mean; each line should reveal character, advance plot, create conflict, or carry subtext. [Novelry](https://www.thenovelry.com/blog/show-dont-tell)

### Inferences
- The strongest AI fiction tells are not single words. They are convergence: the same names, settings, theme statements, and tidy endings across many outputs. Human readers notice this as "generic" more than as "AI."
- Name and setting clichés are a symptom of the same mode collapse measured in the diversity papers. Swapping names alone does not fix the story.
- Explicit theme statements and missing subplots suggest the model is resolving meaning and conflict in the text instead of leaving it for the reader.
- Poetry evidence is weaker than fiction evidence: the best-known test used an old model, and I found no 2025+ study on poetry or lyric tells.

### Gaps
- Lyrics: no research or editor source found. Forced rhyme, abstract nouns, and moral last stanzas are practitioner observations only, unsourced here.
- Gardner (The Art of Fiction), King (On Writing), and Save the Cat critiques: not retrieved. Craft principles below rely on the thin sources above and need checking against the books.
- Specific cliché list ("tapestry," "whispers," "shivers down spines," "a testament to"): the Wikipedia guide covers "tapestry," "testament," and "pivotal"; the rest is unsourced.
- Full StoryScope and Hamilton & Mimno structural numbers: only abstracts or summaries read. Verify before quoting.
- "Revision passes that work" with evidence: no study found. The passes below are practitioner advice.

## Tells (fiction, screenwriting, poetry, lyrics)

- **Tell:** Stock names (Elara, Elias, Mara, Kael, Lyra, Thorne).
  **Why it reads as AI:** Measured: 11 words cover 88% of 20,000 LLM stories; these names are rare in published fiction. [arXiv 2605.26492](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26492)
  **Fix / craft move:** Pick names from the character's world (age, place, family, class). Test by asking "who names their kid this?" Use an unusual surname, a nickname, or a name that is wrong for the character.
  **Source:** as above.

- **Tell:** Stock settings: lighthouse, clockmaker's shop, bakery, library, small coastal town, "keeper" professions.
  **Why:** Same measured convergence; setting carries no specific detail.
  **Fix:** Start from a place you can describe with three objects the reader would not expect. Ask what job the character does that the reader has not seen in fiction.
  **Source:** [Cornell Chronicle, 19 Oct 2026](https://news.cornell.edu/node/333804)

- **Tell:** Stated theme. Characters or narrator say what the story means ("she realized love is about letting go").
  **Why:** StoryScope (summary): 77% of AI stories state their theme vs 52% of human stories.
  **Fix:** Cut the summarizing line. Let an action or object carry the meaning, and trust a single image to do it.
  **Source:** [TDS summary of StoryScope](https://towardsdatascience.com/what-makes-ai-written-stories-feel-off)

- **Tell:** Characters state their feelings. "I'm angry." "I was afraid."
  **Why:** Removes subtext; no conflict between what is said and meant.
  **Fix:** Have the character deflect, change the subject, or overdo a small action. "I'm not angry" read as admission. Revise to remove the feeling word and keep the behavior.
  **Source:** [Novelry](https://www.thenovelry.com/blog/show-dont-tell)

- **Tell:** No subplot; one thread, no secondary pressure.
  **Why:** StoryScope (summary): 79% of AI stories had none vs 57% of human stories.
  **Fix:** Give a secondary character a want that runs against the protagonist's, even if it stays in the background.
  **Source:** [TDS summary](https://towardsdatascience.com/what-makes-ai-written-stories-feel-off)

- **Tell:** Tidy moral or resolution; conflict resolved fast and cleanly.
  **Why:** Matches the "tidy ending" pattern in Wikipedia's "Formulaic challenges sections" tell applied to fiction; a practitioner observation, not measured in these sources.
  **Fix:** Let the ending cost something. Leave one question open that the character would rather not answer. Stop the scene one beat earlier than feels comfortable.

- **Tell:** Everyone speaks in the same voice, with polished grammar and complete sentences.
  **Why:** Uniform voice is the diversity problem applied to dialogue. Wikipedia lists uniform rhetorical patterns (rule of three, "not X but Y") as tells in expository text.
  **Fix:** Give each character a rhythm and vocabulary: interruptions, fragments, wrong words, habits of speech. Read each speaker's lines aloud and check they could not be swapped.
  **Source:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)

- **Tell:** "Not X, but Y" constructions and rule-of-three lists.
  **Why:** Wikipedia guide lists them as common LLM patterns: negative parallelisms argue against a misconception nobody stated.
  **Fix:** Say the positive claim once. Cut the foil. Break three-part lists to two or four items when the real count is different.
  **Source:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)

- **Tell:** Uniform sentence rhythm and clause length.
  **Why:** Wikipedia and editor reports note "polished but flat" prose; a practitioner pattern, measured as a tell in the editor reports only loosely.
  **Fix:** Vary sentence length on purpose. Use one very short line where the emotion peaks. Let a long sentence run when the character's thought runs.

- **Tell:** Overused vocabulary: "tapestry," "testament," "delve," "pivotal," "crucial," "underscore."
  **Why:** Wikipedia's guide lists them as clustering words in LLM text.
  **Fix:** Replace with the concrete noun or verb the author would use on a bad day. Ask what the object or action is.
  **Source:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)

- **Tell:** Em-dash overuse.
  **Why:** Listed in Wikipedia's guide as a historical signal (2022 to Sept 2026). Weak alone.
  **Fix:** Use commas, full stops, or restructure. Use dashes only where an interruption is the point.

- **Tell:** Generic, abstract imagery in poetry and lyrics ("the weight of silence," "endless night," "a river of tears").
  **Why:** Practitioner observation; no 2025+ study found.
  **Fix:** Replace the abstract noun with one object the speaker can touch. Ask: what does the speaker see, hear, or smell right now?

- **Tell:** Moralizing final stanza or verse; the poem explains its lesson.
  **Why:** Practitioner observation; the same "stated meaning" pattern as fiction.
  **Fix:** End on the image, not the explanation. Cut the last stanza and check whether the poem is better.

- **Tell:** Forced rhyme (rhyme chosen over meaning; inverted syntax to fit the rhyme).
  **Why:** Practitioner observation.
  **Fix:** Rewrite the line in plain word order first. Accept slant rhyme or none where the rhyme forces the sense.

## Craft principles

1. **Specificity beats vocabulary.** A generic setting and generic name are the same mistake. A real detail (a brand, a repair, a local word, a number) signals that someone looked. Build the world from what the character would notice, not from what the genre expects.
2. **Subtext over statement.** Characters do not say what they mean. Let them avoid, joke, or overreact. Each line should do one job: reveal character, advance plot, create conflict, or carry subtext. Dialogue that only transfers information is the most common failure.
3. **Show, don't tell, but don't banish telling.** Le Guin treats the maxim as a rule of thumb. Exposition is fine when it moves the story and is brief. Delete the sentence that tells the reader what the scene meant.
4. **Trust the reader.** Leave a gap for the reader to fill (Friedman's framing). Do not close every thread or state the theme. The meaning should come from what the reader infers.
5. **Make conflict cost something.** Resolutions that arrive too easily read as artificial. Let a choice have a loss. Keep at least one want unmet.
6. **Voice is many small choices.** Give each character habits: rhythm, vocabulary, what they avoid saying. Test by swapping names in a dialogue exchange; if nothing changes, the voice is missing.
7. **Structure with surprise.** Secondary threads, a reversal, a scene that goes somewhere the premise did not promise. Stories that run a single thread at one pitch feel generated.
8. **Revise by subtraction.** Cut stated feelings, stated themes, final explanatory lines, and every "not X but Y" that has no real foil. Then re-read for specific nouns and speaker-specific lines.
9. **Read aloud.** Dialogue and poetry fail in the mouth first. Check for rhythm monotony, forced rhyme, and any line that sounds like a greeting card.
10. **Mind the editor.** Magazines reject for generic work and for AI-looking work alike, and their detection tools are unreliable (editors say so). The defence is craft, not disguise: a specific, idiosyncratic draft is what editors say they want.
