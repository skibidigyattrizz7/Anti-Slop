The third dryer shuddered and went still, the way the other two had since Tuesday. Fiona pressed her palm flat against its door as if that might help.

"Out again?" Dev said from the bench by the window. He had a paperback open on his knee and a basket of towels beside him, folded into neat squares he never seemed to hurry.

"Out for good, maybe." She took the handwritten sign from the drawer, the one that said *Machines 4, 6 & 7 Off, Sorry!*, and started to tape it up. The tape wouldn't stick to the enamel. She tried again.

Eight machines had been eight machines when her mum opened the place in 1994. Now it was five, and at 2 a.m. the hum of the working ones sounded like a question nobody wanted to answer.

Dev had come in every Tuesday and Friday for six years, always after his shift at the bakery on Byres Road, always with the same battered holdall. He knew the cracked tile by the door. He knew she kept Murray Mints in the till. He'd once fixed the coin slot with a butter knife and wouldn't take a pound for it.

The offer letter sat under the till, unsigned. *Completion Friday.* A chain of wash-and-dry places from Edinburgh, polite men in good coats who talked about "footfall" and "upgrade potential" and never once asked what the place smelled like at this hour.

"You all right, Fi?"

"Fine." She smoothed the sign flat. "Just tired."

He turned a page he hadn't read. Outside, a bus groaned past, its windows empty and lit like an aquarium.

She thought about the Friday after next, the keys, the ring of them in her palm. She thought about how Dev would find out, whether from the new sign on the door or from someone else's mouth, and how much worse the second would be.

"Dev," she said.

He looked up, mild and expectant, and the word she'd been holding stayed where it was.

"Nothing," she said. "Mind the dryer. It bites."
