The third machine had a sign on it in Fiona's capitals, OUT OF ORDER, and under that someone had written AGAIN in biro. Fiona had written that herself on Tuesday.

Dev came in at ten past two with a holdall the size of a body. "Still got the seven?"

"Five," she said. "Two are being fickle."

He loaded his whites into number four, the one with the dent like a thumbprint, and fed it coins from a Tunnock's tin. He counted them twice, lips moving. Eleven pound forty. She knew because she had watched him do it every Thursday for four years.

The sale papers had come through at six. Friday, the solicitor said. Sign at the bank, keys by lunch. The envelope sat in the drawer under lost property, beside a child's mitten and a bank card belonging to someone called Shona.

"You're quiet," Dev said.

"I'm always quiet at two."

"Naw, you're quiet quiet." He dragged a plastic chair round and sat with his knees up, the way he did when he meant to stay. "My mum says the pensioner's chair is getting a cushion. She's bringing one."

"Tell her no cushion."

"She'll bring one anyway."

Number six thumped into its spin. Number three sat dead with its door open, a wet towel hung over the rim as if someone might come back for it. Twenty-two years, and she could still name the man who'd sold her the first one, and not one thing about the one who'd sold her the business.

"Dev. If somebody took this place on..."

"Somebody's taking it on?" He grinned, then saw her face and stopped. "Fi."

She looked at the mitten. It had been in the drawer since March.

"Does the dryer still stop at forty minutes?" she said.

"It's always done that."

"Aye. Right."

Number six buzzed. Dev stood and started folding his whites into the holdall, slow, the way he did everything, leaving room for her to say the rest. She had the words ready. She opened her mouth.
