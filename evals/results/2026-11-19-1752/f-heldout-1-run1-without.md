The fluorescent tube over the folding table buzzed like it had opinions. Fiona counted the machines again. Eight drums in a row behind the glass. Three of them dead, each with a hand-lettered OUT OF ORDER sign taped on, the tape already curling at the corners.

Dev came in at ten past two, same as every Tuesday, a holdall of work shirts over one shoulder. He nodded at her and went straight for machine five.

"Still going," he said, patting the lid.

"Still going," she said.

She watched him sort his whites into the drum with the care of a man doing something he'd been taught by his mother. Ten years he'd been coming in. He knew the wobble of number two, and the way the coin slot on seven ate fifty pences. He'd once brought her a sponge cake on her birthday and never mentioned it again.

The estate agent's email was in her coat pocket. Friday, the buyer's solicitor had said, as if Friday were a small word.

"Quiet tonight," Dev said.

"Aye."

"Your machines are looking tired."

She laughed, short. "Everything is."

He pulled a folded paper from his jacket and smoothed it flat on the table. A flyer: someone's cat, missing near Byres Road. He wanted to know if she'd put it in the window. She said she would. She took it and held it a moment longer than she needed to.

Behind the glass, five drums turned. Three sat with their doors shut and their notices faded. She could fix one, maybe. She could not fix all three, and she could not fix Friday.

"Dev."

He looked up, eyebrows raised, a sock in his hand.

She opened her mouth. A bus sighed at the stop outside and pulled away. The light over the folding table kept buzzing, and the question sat between them in the warm, lint-smelling air, waiting to be picked up or left where it lay.
