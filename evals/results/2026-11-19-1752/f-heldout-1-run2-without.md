The clock above the dryers said ten to two, though Fiona had stopped trusting it years ago. Eight machines, three dead. Handwritten signs on numbers five, six and seven, OUT OF ORDER in her own capitals, until the place looked like it was apologising to itself.

Dev came in at quarter past, bin bag over his shoulder, hood up against drizzle that never committed to rain.

"Usual?" she said.

"Aye, if there's a usual left." He looked at the signs. "Three?"

"Three."

"Christ, Fi. That's the big one. The one that does duvets."

"I know which one it is."

He loaded his washing into the only working drum by the window, the one that rattled on spin. She watched his hands. He fed coins into the slot, counting under his breath.

Friday. The solicitor's letter sat in the till drawer, folded in three. On Friday the papers would be signed, and a couple from Bearsden with a spreadsheet and a plan for craft beer and co-working would take the keys. They'd probably rip the machines out. They'd probably keep the sign about the Wi-Fi password.

"You're quiet," Dev said.

"Am I?"

"Quieter than usual, and that's saying something."

The dryer started its slow turn. Outside, a bus sighed at the stop, and the rain finally decided to be rain.

She opened her mouth. Closed it. Picked up the cloth and wiped the counter, which was already clean.

"Fi."

"There's a sock in number two," she said. "Mind you don't get it wrapped."

He gave her the look he saved for tea he suspected was too strong. Then he turned back to his washing. The drum began to churn, the fluorescent light hummed its one flat note, and she kept her hands busy and her mouth shut while the clock above the dryers, wrong as ever, ticked toward two.
