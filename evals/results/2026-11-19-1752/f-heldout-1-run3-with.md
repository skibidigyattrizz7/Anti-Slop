Number five had taken fifty pence and given back nothing since Tuesday, so Fiona wrote OUT OF ORDER on A4 and blu-tacked it to the glass. Nobody read anything at 2 a.m. except the man who slept on the bench by the dryers.

Dev came in at ten past, carrying a bag of towels the size of a sleeping cat. He looked at the three dead machines before he looked at her.

"Five's gone, then."

"Five, seven and the big one at the back." She counted them off on the counter. "Eight's fine. Eight's always fine."

"You're a liar about eight. I put my jeans in eight last month and they came out the colour of a bruise."

"That was the jeans' fault."

He loaded four, two, six, the same machines he always used. He fed coins in slowly and said, "Sandra's got the dog at the vet again." It was not a question, and she didn't answer it.

The estate agent's email sat unread in her pocket. Offers by Friday. A couple from Edinburgh, a chain, a man who wanted it for vapes and had said so like he was mentioning the weather. Dev had been coming here nine years. He once fixed the coin mech with a bent teaspoon and wouldn't take a pound.

"You're quiet," he said.

"I'm up at two."

"You're always up at two."

She could tell him now. Pull the sheet off the glass and say it: the sale, the new people who would probably paint over the Clyde mural he'd sat under for nine years. Or she could let him find the sign on the door on Friday like everyone else.

The dryer thudded. Dev lifted the lid of six, then four, checking, as if the washing might have changed its mind.

"Fiona."

"Mm."

"Nothing." He pushed the door shut with his hip. "Just wanted to hear you say it."
