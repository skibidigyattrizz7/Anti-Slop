The sign on machine four says OUT OF ORDER in Fiona's biro, the same biro she uses for the float book. Seven has one too, and eight. The other five hum along without them.

Dev comes in at ten past two with a bag of whites under one arm and his phone in the other hand. He nods. She nods back.

"Three down," she says. "Use the dryer on six, it's warm."

"Cheers, Fi." He counts along the row, then sorts at the folding table: socks, shirts, a football top he folds like a flag. Tuesdays and Thursdays, four years. She has never asked what job has him up at this hour.

Behind the counter the solicitor's envelope sits under the float tin. Friday, eleven o'clock. The buyer wants to keep the name. Wants the machines too, what's left of them, and a card reader where the coin slots are.

Dev feeds six. The drum turns.

"Here, Friday," he says. "Callum's wedding's the Saturday. I've the good suit and the big duvet cover. Keep eight for me? Eleven-ish."

"Eight's dead."

"Is it? I thought that was seven."

"Both."

"Six, then. I'll wait."

"I might not be in."

"You're always in." He says it like the weather. "Where would you be?"

She opens the float tin and counts it, though she knows the number. It is forty-one pounds sixty and has been since midnight. She is ashamed, and it's the wrong size of shame for a duvet cover.

"Put me in the diary, if you're worried," he says, and pushes it across the counter, the page for Friday already open, nothing on it. Nobody has ever booked a machine here.

She uncaps the biro. Out on Duke Street a bus goes by, empty and lit up.

"What time did you say?" she asks.

"Eleven."

The nib is on the page. Dev leans on the counter to watch her write it.
