The third machine quit at ten past two, the drum sliding to a stop like it had remembered something. Fiona pressed her palm flat against the door and felt nothing turn.

"Is that you or the machine?" Dev said from the bench. He had a basket on his knee and a paperback face down on top of it, spine cracked white.

"The machine. Two and five have been dead since August. I've a man coming Thursday to look at the lot."

"Thursday's alright."

She took the cardboard sign from the drawer, the one that said OUT OF ORDER in her own capitals, and wedged it against the glass. That made three of eight. She turned it so the corner faced out.

Dev watched her do it. He had a way of watching that made her check her hands. He'd been coming since the old change machine ate pound coins and gave back Polo mints. Once he'd told her his wife's socks had gone missing for a year and then turned up in the dryer at the Cowcaddens branch, and she had believed him, because he was the sort of man who kept track of socks.

"Friday's the surveyor," she said.

"Aye? Mortgage?"

"Something like that. Nothing to worry about."

He nodded at the dryers, the way people nod at weather. "You'll sort it. You always do."

The clock over the door said 2:14. Outside, a van reversed with its alarm going, slow and patient. Dev put his hand on the basket and didn't lift it.

"Fi," he said. "Is there something—"

"Your towel's in four," she said. "Mind it's warm."

He looked at four. He looked at her. Then he stood, took the basket, and went to fold, and she stayed by the sign with Friday in her head and her phone in her apron pocket, face down, buzzing once and then not again.
