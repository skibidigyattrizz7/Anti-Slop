The sign on machine four says OUT OF ORDER in Fiona's biro, the same biro she uses for everything. At two in the morning, the hum of the other five sounds like an apology.

Dev comes in at ten past, the way he always does, a bag of whites under one arm and his phone in the other hand. He nods. She nods back. Nobody in Glasgow needs more than that at this hour.

"Seven's knackered as well," she says.

"Ah." He looks at the drum, then the two dead ones beside it, then at her. "That's three."

"That's three."

He sets the bag on the folding table and starts sorting: socks in one pile, shirts in another. He has done this every Tuesday and Thursday for four years. She watches him fold a football top with the care of a man who has never once been thanked for it.

Behind the counter, the envelope from the solicitor sits under the float tin. Friday, eleven o'clock. The buyer wants to keep the name. Wants the machines too, or what's left of them.

"Dryer's warm, mind," she says. "Use six."

"Cheers, Fi." He feeds in coins. The drum starts turning, slow and sure.

She could tell him now. It would take a sentence, maybe two. The place is going, the lease is gone, and the new people will be bleach and bright lights and a card reader that doesn't take coins. Dev would say something kind and go home.

Instead she counts the float again, though she knows the number. Out on Duke Street, a bus passes, empty and lit like an aquarium.

"You all right?" Dev asks.

"Aye," she says. "Just tired."

He nods. The machine goes round. Neither of them moves to leave.
