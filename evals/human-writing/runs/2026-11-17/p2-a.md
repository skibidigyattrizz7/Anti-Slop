# Why the Sky Is Blue

Sunlight looks white, but it is made of every color in the rainbow. When it reaches the air, it strikes gas molecules, mostly nitrogen and oxygen, and bounces off in all directions. Physicists call this Rayleigh scattering, after Lord Rayleigh, who described it in the 1870s.

How much a color scatters depends on its wavelength. Blue light has a wavelength of about 450 nanometers, and red light about 700. Blue scatters roughly six times more strongly than red, so blue light reaches your eyes from every part of the sky.

You can see this at home. Stir a spoonful of milk into a glass of water and shine a flashlight through it in a dark room. From the side, the liquid looks faintly blue. Look at the light coming out the far end and it looks orange. Same light, different angle.

Violet scatters even more than blue, so why isn't the sky violet? Two reasons. The sun gives off less violet than blue, and our eyes respond to violet weakly. The mix that reaches us looks blue.

Sunsets follow the same rule. At dusk, sunlight crosses far more air before it reaches you. Most of the blue is scattered out along the way, and the reds and oranges are what is left.

Space has almost no air, so nothing scatters the light. Astronauts on the Moon saw a black sky even in full daylight. The blue you see overhead is sunlight that the air has pushed around on its way down to you.
