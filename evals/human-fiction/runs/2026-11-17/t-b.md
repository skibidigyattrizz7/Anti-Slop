The yard lights were still on when Bisi pulled her bus through the gate, and the sky over Oshodi had not yet decided what colour it wanted to be. Tunde sat at the wooden table by the fuel store with the ledger open and a flask of tea going cold beside it. He was fifty-two and had been dispatching these buses since before the gate was painted.

"Eleven," he said, without looking up. "Eleven buses in the yard."

"Eleven," she agreed. "Nine that go."

He wrote something down. The pen was the cheap blue kind that skipped on the downstroke, and he pressed harder to make it take. "Number seven has the gearbox. Number four, the radiator again. The mechanic says Friday."

"The mechanic says Friday every Friday."

Behind them a generator coughed and caught. Bisi leaned against the bonnet of her bus. She had not taken her cap off. Three weeks. She did not need to say the number, and she could see him knowing it, the way his shoulders went up and stayed there.

"Tunde."

"I know."

"You know what?"

He closed the ledger. Then he opened it again, to a page she could not read from where she stood, and ran his finger down a column that was mostly crossed out. "The company paid the fuel man on Monday. The fuel man has a brother who drives the Ikeja route, and the Ikeja route is making money, so..." He stopped. "I put your name at the top. I wrote it myself."

"At the top of what?"

"The list."

"What list has me at the top, Tunde? The one where the money comes after the fuel?"

He didn't answer that. A bus engine turned over somewhere in the yard, and both of them listened until it settled into its ordinary rough idle, which was not the sound of anything being fixed.

She thought of her daughter's school fees, due Thursday, and did not say it. She thought of the other drivers, who were not here yet, and who would ask her what he had said.

"Madam Ngozi is coming at six," he said. "The owner. She will want the sheet."

"Then give her the sheet."

"It shows eleven buses. It does not show nine."

Bisi took off her cap at last and turned it over in her hands. Outside the gate, the first commuters were gathering under the flyover, bags held close, eyes on the road for any yellow shape that might stop.

"Tunde," she said again.

He picked up the pen. He put it down. The light was coming up grey over the fence, the flask was empty, and neither of them moved toward the door.
