The gate was already half up when Bisi arrived, which meant Tunde had been there long enough to open it and not long enough to feel sorry about anything. Generator noise. Diesel on the concrete. Eleven buses in two rows, nose to the wall, and two at the far end with their bonnets up.

"Seven and nine," Tunde said, without turning round. The clipboard sat against his stomach. "Gearbox, and the other thing. Ayo says Thursday."

"Ayo said Thursday last Thursday."

"Then he is consistent."

Bisi dropped her bag on the bench by the tyre rack. Someone had cut her initials into the wood years ago and nobody had admitted to it since. She waited. Tunde kept writing. The pen scratched like a fingernail on a tin.

"Ibadan run is yours at six," he said. "Nine is out, so you take the old Toyota."

"The old Toyota pulls left."

"Then hold it left."

A conductor came in through the side gate with bread in a paper bag, saw the room, and stopped chewing. He was about nineteen and had a new phone with a cracked corner. He went to the far bench and sat very upright.

"Three weeks," Bisi said.

The pen stopped. Tunde did not look up. "The fares went down on that route. Fuel went up. It is coming."

"I have a daughter with a school fee on the twentieth."

"I know her school fee."

"Then you know the twentieth."

She could see him reaching for the old voice, the one he used on drivers who came in smelling of gin. It did not arrive. He turned the clipboard over as if the back might have better numbers.

"Sell the tyres off nine," he said. "Ayo will give you something."

"You want me to sell your buses."

"The company's buses."

"You are the company at five in the morning, Tunde."

The generator coughed and caught. On the road a danfo horn sounded twice, somebody's joke. The conductor pretended to read his phone.

"Six o'clock," Tunde said at last. He slid the sheet across the desk, pen on top, and tapped the line where her name was. She looked at the pen for a long time.
